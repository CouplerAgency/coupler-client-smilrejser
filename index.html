Where the money actually leaks

Both of those big drops sit exactly where our scripted walkthrough got stuck: step 1, which opens in an error state, and step 3, which asks for 27 fields and rejects them in English. Two things to hold in mind when reading GA4 here

Revenue is credited to the session's landing page, so those figures describe entry points, not persuasion. And the checkout emits no document.title, so every funnel number is keyed on URL path — GA4's own page-title reports show the whole checkout as one blank row.

One note on tooling

Screaming Frog was the plan, but the local licence is inactive and the free tier has no CLI, no rendering and no export. We wrote a crawler instead, which captures CRO-specific fields Screaming Frog has no column for — such as whether a departure is actually bookable. Its one real gap, internal link structure, we closed separately: the Internal links row above.

A correction we owe you

Our first-pass crawler flagged 103 editorial pages as “claiming to be products”. That was wrong, and re-checking is how we found the real bug. The Product entries are list items describing linked trips — correct, conventional markup for a listing page. Only the list's name is broken.

Why it matters

This is the data Google uses to understand what a page sells, and every destination page tells it the same wrong thing. numberOfItems and the item names are correct, so one string carries the whole defect.

The fix — one line

Interpolate the destination the page is already rendering in its own H1. A hardcoded default that happens to say “Frankrig” suggests this was written against a France page and never parameterised.

The convergence is the point

We walked this funnel before we had any analytics, and got stuck twice: once at step 1, because travellers defaults to 1 while both room counters default to 0, and once at step 3, because required fields reject input without saying which. Those are precisely the two steps where GA4 shows 71% and 69% of users leaving. The screenshots on the next two slides are photographs of where the revenue goes.

Why step 5 is not a 47% drop

Confirmation shows more users than the payment page — 159 against 117 — which cannot happen in a strictly linear funnel. Payment is almost certainly handled off-site, with customers returning directly to confirmation. So /payment is a partial route, not a stage, and the apparent drop there should not be acted on until the dev team confirms the flow.

Not all 71% is a defect

Some visitors press Bestil simply to see the price, with no intention of buying. But step 1 averages 1.63 views per user and the summary step 1.71, which is the signature of people submitting a form more than once. A share of that 71% is browsing; a share of it is being turned away.

Finding 4 · Critical, and a five-line fix

Step 1 of checkout cannot be submitted as it is presented

Travellers defaults to 1. Both room counters default to 0. Those two facts contradict each other, so every single customer hits an error on the first click — and only finds out after pressing the button.

The error, verbatim

Der opstod en fejl Antallet af værelser og personer matcher ikke. Ret venligst til korrekt antal for at komme videre med din booking. // "An error occurred. The number of // rooms and people do not match. // Please correct to the right number // to continue with your booking."
Verbatim from the live funnel, 26.08.2026.
The fix — under an hour

Default one traveller to one single room. Better still, disable Videre until the counts reconcile and show the reason inline next to the room picker, so the customer never reaches a red error at all.

Two more things on this step

Cancel is offered twice — “Annuller booking” top-left and “Annuller” bottom-left — while the primary action is a modest green button. And the +/− counters have no text and no aria-label, so they are unusable by screen reader or voice control.

Worth saying out loud

This is the single cheapest item in the audit. It sits on the first step of the only path to revenue, it affects every booking attempt, and it is a default-value change.

The two insurances

“Afbestillings- og rejseforsikring” sits directly above “Afbestillingsforsikring”, both defaulting to “Nej, tak”. One word tells them apart, so most will take neither.

The room naming

The customer selects Enkeltværelse (single room). The summary bills it as “Dobbeltværelse til 1 person +995 DKK” (double room for one person). Same thing, two names, at the moment of payment.

Fixes, in order of cheapness

Translate the validation strings. Add real required attributes. Split step 3 into traveller and booker. Move the discount field to the summary. Name the room the same way twice.

What to do with this

Travel-type and destination-hub pages together take 10.6% of sessions and are the two best-performing entry points on the site after the homepage. They are also where paid and organic acquisition is easiest to point. Before building anything new, we would test moving acquisition spend from individual trip pages towards /kulturrejser, /historiske-rejser and the country hubs, and measure it against this baseline.

The blog earns nothing at all

65 posts, 4,127 sessions, 6.4% of all traffic, zero revenue and zero key events across 90 days. It also has the lowest engagement on the site at 64 seconds against 204 on the homepage.

The link graph explains part of it: a blog post carries a median of one in-content inbound link, and posts rarely link forward into bookable inventory. Traffic arrives, reads, and leaves.

Why we are not recommending deleting it

Zero attributed revenue is not the same as zero value — last-click attribution on a 90-day window will miss a reader who returns two months later to book. What it does establish is that the blog is not currently a conversion asset, and that adding trip links to the highest-traffic posts is a cheaper experiment than commissioning more posts.

One page, 432 wasted sessions

/italien/trentino-og-dolomitterne returns 404 and still took 432 sessions in the window. Nineteen other dead URLs have traffic, but one or two sessions each — a single redirect fixes 86% of the problem. Full list in evidence/dead_urls.csv.

Before the recommendations

Four things we went looking for and found already done properly

This is not padding. It changes what we recommend, because the foundations are sound and the fixes are configuration rather than rebuild. Any agency telling you this site needs replacing has not looked at it.

STRENGTH 1
Server-side tagging, live and healthy

A shared container serves ten Aller travel brands, routing each hostname to its own GA4 property and its own server-side endpoint. Smilrejser's is sgtm.smilrejser.dk, which answered /healthy with HTTP 200 in 0.22 s. Consent Mode is wired with wait_for_update and ads_data_redaction, and enhanced-conversion PII is SHA-256 hashed client-side.

STRENGTH 2
The full ecommerce funnel is already configured

view_item, add_to_cart, begin_checkout, add_payment_info, add_shipping_info and purchase all exist as configured GA4 tags — plus purchase_giftcard, waitinglist_form_submit, site_search, click_tel and click_mail. The waitlist is already tracked, so the empty-departure problem is measurable today without any new tagging.

STRENGTH 3
Structured data is genuinely well built

Every trip page emits Product, AggregateOffer and BreadcrumbList, and 56 add FAQPage. Destination pages correctly emit Collection and TouristDestination instead — the page types are properly distinguished. Better than most travel sites we audit; finding 3 is one wrong string inside otherwise sound markup.

STRENGTH 4
Trust and price transparency

Trustpilot, Rejsegarantifonden and Travelife appear on all 421 pages, and a phone number is on every one of them. Checkout shows the administration fee as a separate line and a true total. All 421 URLs returned HTTP 200 with a canonical tag and exactly one H1 — no broken pages, no redirect chains.

And one thing we suspected, checked, and were wrong about

Our first screenshots showed the cookie banner in Norwegian on a Danish site, which looked like a significant defect. It was not: Cookiebot picks its language from the browser, and the Norwegian text came from this Mac's own locale. Re-run with a Danish locale, the banner is correct Danish. It is in the evidence log as a rejected finding, and every screenshot in this deck was re-captured with a Danish locale.

Housekeeping we found

Nine Universal Analytics Enhanced Ecommerce variables are still in the container — ecommerce.purchase.actionField.revenue and siblings. Ordered so the cheapest fixes ship first, against the steps where GA4 shows the losses, and nothing expensive is decided before we can measure whether the cheap work moved anything.

Result: the first round of decisions made against a measured before-and-after rather than a ranking.

One thing we want to be explicit about

The GA4 window is 90 days of one Danish summer, and it contains 201 key events. That is enough to rank groups of pages and to read a funnel with confidence; it is not enough to quote a conversion rate for any single page, and we have quoted none. It also means the honest test of phase 1 is a before-and-after on the funnel steps themselves, not a revenue attribution — which is exactly why action 3, setting page titles, is in the first week rather than the last.
